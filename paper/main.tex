\begin{abstract}
Self-supervised models promise one representation, pre-trained once on unlabelled survey data and
read out cheaply for many downstream tasks. In stellar time-domain astronomy that promise competes
with a strong, cheap incumbent: a few dozen hand-engineered light-curve statistics. We train a
Conv1D variational encoder with a recurrent latent-dynamics model on 195{,}883 unlabelled TESS
two-minute light curves, freeze it, and evaluate the resulting star embedding on ten labelled tasks
spanning variability classification, asteroseismology and rotation, against three baselines: the
engineered features alone, an architecture-matched supervised Conv1D trained end-to-end, and an MLP
on raw flux. Concatenating the learned embedding with the engineered features improves a frozen
linear readout beyond $2\,\mathrm{SE}$ on 7 of 10 tasks, while an untrained encoder does not. The
same frozen pipeline is never beaten by the supervised Conv1D (ahead on 6, tied on 4, behind on 0)
and beats the raw-flux MLP on 8 of 10. \textbf{A learned representation and hand-built features are
complementary rather than competing, and once combined, plain logistic regression on frozen
features matches what end-to-end deep networks reach on the labels that exist.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Wide-field photometric surveys now produce light curves far faster than they can be labelled. TESS
\citep{ricker2015} has observed hundreds of thousands of bright stars at two-minute cadence, and
LSST \citep{ivezic2019} will enlarge the gap by orders of magnitude. Self-supervised learning is the
natural response: pre-train one encoder on the unlabelled archive, then attach a cheap readout for
each scientific question. This shape has recently been pursued for astronomical light curves by
\citet{donoso2025astromer2}, \citet{zuo2025falco} and \citet{rui2026jepa}, and for TESS by
\citet{poznanski2026}, who validates with embedding diagnostics rather than downstream tasks.

What makes this domain unusual is the strength of the incumbent it must beat. Decades of variable-star
research have distilled light curves into compact engineered summaries -- periodogram peak structure,
band-limited power, spectral entropy, robust scatter statistics -- and pipelines built on them remain
highly competitive \citep{audenaert2021}. The benchmark of \citet{li2025staremb} makes the point
sharply: across seven expert-labelled variable-star classes, no time-series foundation model they
tested strictly surpassed the long-established domain feature baseline on classification. A learned
representation for this data therefore has to answer a specific question, not a generic one: does it
carry information the engineered features do not already have?

We take that question literally and measure it. We pre-train a latent-dynamics encoder on unlabelled
TESS photometry, freeze it, and ask what its embedding adds to a 25-feature engineered stack under an
identical linear readout -- and whether the resulting pipeline, which trains no deep network at
readout time, is competitive with deep networks trained end-to-end on the same labels. Our
contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The learned embedding is complementary to engineered features, not a replacement for
them.} Concatenating the two improves a frozen linear readout beyond $2\,\mathrm{SE}$ on 7 of 10
tasks, with the gains concentrated where the engineered basis is information-starved; an
untrained-encoder control shows the effect is not generic column addition.
\item \textbf{The combined pipeline, read out by logistic regression, is not beaten by end-to-end
deep learning on these labels.} Against an architecture-matched supervised Conv1D it is ahead on 6
of 10 tasks, tied on 4 and behind on none; against an MLP on raw flux it is ahead on 8 of 10.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data and method}

\paragraph{Data.} We use TESS two-minute SPOC PDCSAP photometry for 195{,}883 stars brighter than
$T = 10$. Each light curve is split into contiguous segments at runs of missing cadences and at time
gaps exceeding five times the median cadence; segments are median-normalised by their median absolute
deviation and sliced into non-overlapping windows of 256 cadences ($\approx 8.5$\,h). Missing values
are never interpolated or padded -- any window containing one is discarded, and segments are never
stitched across sector or gap boundaries. Pre-training consumes sequences of 16 consecutive windows
($\approx 5.69$\,d) and uses no labels of any kind.

\paragraph{Encoder.} A 1D convolutional variational encoder \citep{kingma2014} maps each window to a
128-dimensional Gaussian posterior, and a GRU \citep{cho2014} predicts the next window's latent from
its predecessors, run both forward and backward over the sequence. The training objective combines
window reconstruction, the KL term, the latent-dynamics prediction error, and a Hann-tapered spectral
auxiliary term that penalises reconstruction error in the log periodogram. The encoder is trained
once with six random seeds and then frozen; no gradient from any label ever reaches it.

\paragraph{Representations and readout.} A star is represented by $\mu$, the mean over its first
segment (16--20 windows) of the per-window posterior means. The engineered baseline is a fixed
25-feature vector modelled on the T'DA variability pipeline \citep{audenaert2021}: the three
strongest periodogram peaks with their normalised powers and ratios, a harmonic-power ratio, power in
eight log-spaced frequency bands, spectral entropy and centroid, skew, kurtosis, point-to-point
scatter ratio, and three robust dispersion statistics. Readouts are deterministic and frozen,
standardised on the training split alone: class-balanced $L_{2}$ logistic regression at a fixed
$C = 1$ for the detection tasks, ridge for the two regressions with its penalty set by leave-one-out
cross-validation. Cross-validating $C$ as well changes the reported gains by at most $0.011$, and the
two tasks that move by more than $0.008$ both move in the fusion arm's favour. We report four
arms -- \texttt{features}, $\mu$, \texttt{features}\,$\oplus\,\mu$, and the same fusion built on an
\emph{untrained} encoder as a control.

\paragraph{Supervised baselines.} Two end-to-end baselines see exactly the same input windows, star
sets and splits. \textbf{C1} reuses the encoder's convolutional trunk verbatim, adds a bottleneck and
a scalar head, and is trained per task with a star-level bag loss (the mean of the star's window
logits, taken in-graph) and class weighting. \textbf{C2} replaces the convolutional trunk with a
dense one and changes nothing else, so C1 versus C2 isolates the convolutional inductive bias. Both
use one pre-registered regularisation setting for all tasks, select on a validation split no probe
has ever touched, and are trained with three seeds.

\paragraph{Tasks and metrics.} We score ten tasks with one probe per physical quantity: pulsating
variables \citep{gao2025}, eclipsing binaries \citep{prsa2022}, rotation and rotation period
\citep{boyle2026}, transits \citep{guerrero2021}, flare activity \citep{seli2025,vida2021},
oscillating red giants and their $\nu_{\max}$ \citep{hon2021}, solar-like oscillators
\citep{hatt2023}, and RGB versus helium-burning discrimination \citep{sreenivas2026}. Here
$\nu_{\max}$ is the frequency of maximum oscillation power. Detection tasks are scored by the area
under the precision--recall curve (PR-AUC), whose floor is the positive-class prevalence rather than
$0.5$; prevalence is therefore quoted with every detection score. The two regressions are scored by
$R^{2}$.

\paragraph{Uncertainties.} All error bars are $2\times$ the standard error of the mean over seeds.
For the $\mu$ arms the seeds are independent pre-training runs (six); for C1 and C2 they are
initialisation and shuffling seeds (three, fixed before any run). Because a pre-training seed and an
initialisation seed are different objects, differences between a $\mu$ arm and a supervised arm are
treated as unpaired and their bands added in quadrature; the smaller supervised count widens those
bands, so the asymmetry can only turn our wins into ties. The engineered arm is deterministic and
carries no seed spread.
A difference counts as resolved only when it exceeds the combined $2\,\mathrm{SE}$ band.

\paragraph{The learned embedding adds to the engineered features.} Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} and
Figure~\ref{fig:deltas}a give the comparison. Concatenating $\mu$ with the 25 features improves the
frozen readout beyond $2\,\mathrm{SE}$ on 7 of 10 tasks, led by solar-like oscillators $+0.071$,
pulsating $+0.057$ and flare activity $+0.051$, and reaching $+0.013$ ($R^{2}$) on rotation period.
Rotation is positive but just short of the
bar ($+0.016$ against $0.016$). The two negative tasks are the ones where the engineered basis is
already saturated or the probe is small: oscillating giants ($-0.003$, on a baseline of $0.920$) and
RGB versus helium-burning ($-0.051$, 161 test stars). On both, an untrained-encoder control loses by an amount we cannot resolve from the trained
fusion's -- the signature of extra collinear columns diluting a linear fit, not of $\mu$ carrying
misleading content. Across all ten tasks the untrained control is negative on seven
and never exceeds $+0.016$, so the gains are specific to what pre-training learned. $\mu$ on its own
does not replace the features: it wins on only 4 of 10 tasks alone, which is the same conclusion
\citet{li2025staremb} reach for foundation-model embeddings, and is why we report fusion rather than
substitution \citep{makinen2024}.

\paragraph{The frozen pipeline is not beaten by end-to-end deep learning.} Against C1 -- the same
convolutional trunk, the same labels, the same input windows, trained end-to-end -- the frozen fusion
probe is ahead on 6 of 10 tasks, tied on 4, and behind on none (Figure~\ref{fig:deltas}b). Against
C2, an MLP on raw flux, it is ahead on 8 of 10 and behind on one (RGB versus helium-burning, 161
stars). C1 is not weak: it clears the engineered arm on 6 of 10 and the $\mu$-only arm on 4, and on
eight tasks the frozen probe recovers $0.97$--$1.09$ of its margin above the metric floor. C1 also beats C2 on 8 of 10, failing only on the two smallest training sets (755 and 669 stars),
where the 132k-parameter dense trunk beats the 1.1M-parameter convolutional one on capacity grounds.
The correct reading of this comparison is
\emph{not} that self-supervision matches supervision in general. It matches what this architecture
reaches on the labels that actually exist, which range from 669 to 16{,}002 labelled stars per task.
The deployed object is correspondingly cheap: a new task costs one logistic regression over named
axes, not a training run and a search.

\paragraph{The latent-dynamics term is what buys the complementarity.} Replacing the
forward--backward dynamics objective with a dynamics-free variant, all else fixed and the same six
seeds, reduces the fusion gain on all 10 tasks, and by more than $2\,\mathrm{SE}$ on 6 when the two
arms are differenced seed by seed (Figure~\ref{fig:deltas}a, open triangles; largest losses solar-like
oscillators $-0.047$, rotation $-0.026$, $\nu_{\max}$ $-0.025$). The recurrent prediction term, not
the autoencoder alone, supplies the information the engineered features lack.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Selecting an encoder is harder than training one.} Our largest practical difficulty was
not optimisation but model selection. The reconstruction objective develops a localised artifact at the
window boundary, and none of eleven interventions across four families of fix satisfied both
pre-registered acceptance criteria, so the tapered variant shipped on the stated rule, not because it
won. Underlying this is a
measurement we think generalises. Across ten pre-training
recipes, validation reconstruction \emph{anti}-predicts downstream probe score: the Spearman $\rho$
between negated validation reconstruction and PR-AUC runs from $-0.58$ to $-0.84$ over four tasks, the
loss-optimal recipe is the dynamics-free one on all four, and trusting the loss costs $0.021$--$0.049$
PR-AUC. The effect lives across recipes, not across seeds of one recipe, where no label-free metric we
tried ranks anything. Self-supervised representation work in this domain therefore cannot be tuned on
its own objective and must be gated on a held-out probe.

\paragraph{\textbf{Limitations.}} Our corpus is the bright ($T < 10$) two-minute sample, which is
better studied and less confused than the faint population a survey-scale claim would need. Every arm
sees only each star's first segment ($\approx 5.7$\,d), which equalises access but bounds the
supervised baselines at the probe's input scope rather than in general. Our labels come from incomplete catalogues, so a
model-positive, catalogue-negative star scores as a false positive even when genuine. Rotation-period regression saturates at the 5.69-day input
baseline -- stars with true periods averaging 8.60 and 17.34 days are both answered near 5.6 -- so its
$R^{2}$ is not period inference beyond the segment length. Flare is an activity label, not a detection
label: 81\% of labelled positives show no catalogued flare inside the encoded segment, so we claim
flare propensity only; auditing its negative class against the detector's own published search cut
moved the gain from $+0.0506$ to $+0.0507$. Under this frozen readout the advantage is also a
large-label phenomenon, narrowing on 6 of 10 tasks as the labelled budget falls towards one percent.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and disclosure.} \S2 gives the corpus cut, geometry, features, objective
and readouts; the remaining sizes are four convolutional stages of width $32/64/128/256$, kernel 5,
each halving length, a 128-dimensional Gaussian latent, and a single-layer GRU of width 256 across the
16 windows. Splits are by star, drawn once, and shared byte-for-byte by every arm, including both
supervised baselines, which reproduce the probe's star sets and test counts exactly. Code and score tables
will be released on publication; generative AI assisted with analysis code and prose editing.
