\title{What does self-supervision add to engineered features\\for stellar light curves?}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)\\Affiliation\\Address\\\texttt{email}}

\begin{abstract}
Self-supervised models promise one representation, pre-trained once on unlabelled survey data and
read out cheaply for many downstream tasks. In stellar time-domain astronomy that promise has to
improve on a strong, cheap incumbent: a few dozen engineered light-curve statistics. We pre-train a
Conv1D variational encoder with a recurrent latent-dynamics model on 195{,}883 unlabelled TESS
2-minute light curves, freeze it, and ask whether the features it learns add anything to 25
engineered features under one linear readout, on ten labelled tasks spanning variability
classification, asteroseismology and rotation. Concatenating the two improves the readout beyond
$2\,\mathrm{SE}$ on 7 of 10 tasks; an untrained encoder does not. Against two networks trained
end-to-end on the same labelled stars, splits and inputs, the frozen readout is ahead of a supervised
Conv1D with the encoder's architecture on 6 of 10 tasks and indistinguishable on the other 4, and
ahead of an MLP on raw flux on 9 of 10. \textbf{Self-supervision adds to engineered features rather
than replacing them. Under a plain logistic regression, the combination matches or exceeds the two
supervised networks we train on the same 669 to 16{,}002 labelled stars per task.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Wide-field photometric surveys now produce light curves far faster than they can be labelled. TESS
\citep{ricker2015} trades sampling rate against sky coverage: 2-minute (or 20-second) postage stamps
for hundreds of thousands of pre-selected stars, and full-frame images, now every 200\,s, for every
star in the field. LSST \citep{ivezic2019} will enlarge the gap by orders of magnitude. Self-supervised learning is the
natural response: pre-train one encoder on the unlabelled archive, then attach a cheap readout for
each scientific question. This shape has recently been pursued for astronomical light curves by
\citet{donoso2025astromer2}, \citet{zuo2025falco} and \citet{rui2026jepa}, and for TESS by
\citet{poznanski2026}, who validates with embedding diagnostics rather than downstream tasks.

What makes this domain unusual is the strength of the incumbent it must improve on. Decades of variable-star
research have distilled light curves into compact engineered summaries -- periodogram peak structure,
band-limited power, spectral entropy, robust scatter statistics -- and pipelines built on them remain
highly competitive \citep{audenaert2021}. The benchmark of \citet{li2025staremb} makes the point
sharply: across seven expert-labelled variable-star classes, no time-series foundation model they
tested strictly surpassed the long-established domain feature baseline on classification. A learned
representation for this data therefore has to answer a specific question, not a generic one: does it
carry information the engineered features do not already have?

We take that question literally and measure it. We pre-train a latent-dynamics encoder on unlabelled
TESS photometry, freeze it, and ask what the features it learns add to a 25-feature engineered stack under an
identical linear readout -- and whether the resulting pipeline, which trains no deep network at
readout time, matches or exceeds two networks trained end-to-end on the same labels. Our
contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The learned features are complementary to the engineered ones, not a replacement for
them.} Concatenating the two improves a frozen linear readout beyond $2\,\mathrm{SE}$ on 7 of 10
tasks, with the gains concentrated where the engineered basis is information-starved; an
untrained-encoder control shows the effect is not generic column addition.
\item \textbf{The combined pipeline, read out by logistic regression, matches or exceeds the two
supervised networks we train end-to-end on the same labels.} Against a supervised Conv1D with the
encoder's architecture it is ahead on 6
of 10 tasks and indistinguishable on the other 4; against an MLP on raw flux it is ahead on 9 of 10.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data and method}

\paragraph{Data.} We use the 2-minute SPOC PDCSAP target light curves of 195{,}883 stars brighter
than $T = 10$, one segment per star, drawn from Sectors 1--101; one cadence keeps one window a fixed
span of physical time. Each light curve is split into contiguous segments at runs of missing cadences and at time
gaps exceeding five times the median cadence; segments are median-normalised by their median absolute
deviation and sliced into non-overlapping windows of 256 cadences ($256 \times 120$\,s $\approx 8.5$\,h). Missing values
are never interpolated or padded -- any window containing one is discarded, and segments are never
stitched across sector or gap boundaries. Pre-training consumes sequences of 16 consecutive windows
($\approx 5.69$\,d) and uses no labels of any kind.

\paragraph{Encoder.} A 1D convolutional variational encoder \citep{kingma2014} maps each window to a
128-dimensional Gaussian posterior, and a GRU \citep{cho2014} predicts the next window's latent from
its predecessors, run both forward and backward over the sequence. The training objective combines
window reconstruction, the KL term, the latent-dynamics prediction error, and a Hann-tapered spectral
auxiliary term that penalises reconstruction error in the log periodogram. The encoder is trained
once with six random seeds and then frozen; no gradient from any label ever reaches it.

\paragraph{Representations and readout.} A star's learned features are $\mu$, the mean over its first
segment (16--20 windows) of the per-window posterior means. The engineered baseline is a fixed
25-feature vector modelled on the T'DA variability pipeline \citep{audenaert2021}: the three
strongest periodogram peaks with their normalised powers and ratios, a harmonic-power ratio, power in
eight log-spaced frequency bands, spectral entropy and centroid, skew, kurtosis, point-to-point
scatter ratio, and three robust dispersion statistics. Readouts are deterministic and frozen,
standardised on the training split alone: class-balanced $L_{2}$ logistic regression at a fixed
$C = 1$ for the detection tasks, ridge for the two regressions with its penalty set by leave-one-out
cross-validation (cross-validating $C$ as well moves no reported gain by more than $0.011$). We compare four
arms, each the same readout on a different feature set: \texttt{features}, $\mu$,
\texttt{features}\,$\oplus\,\mu$, and, as a control, the same fusion built on an \emph{untrained}
encoder. The two supervised baselines of the next paragraph are the fifth and sixth arms.

\paragraph{Supervised baselines.} Two end-to-end baselines see exactly the same input windows, star
sets and splits. \textbf{C1} reuses the encoder's convolutional trunk verbatim, adds a bottleneck and
a scalar head, and is trained per task with a star-level bag loss (the mean of the star's window
logits, taken in-graph) and class weighting. \textbf{C2} replaces the convolutional trunk with a
dense one and changes nothing else, so C1 versus C2 isolates the convolutional inductive bias. Both
use one pre-registered regularisation setting for all tasks, select on a validation split no probe
has ever touched, and are trained with six seeds.

\paragraph{Tasks and metrics.} We score ten tasks with one probe per physical quantity: pulsating
variables \citep{gao2025}, eclipsing binaries \citep{prsa2022}, rotation and rotation period
\citep{boyle2026}, transits \citep{guerrero2021}, flare activity \citep{seli2025,vida2021},
oscillating red giants and their $\nu_{\max}$ \citep{hon2021}, solar-like oscillators
\citep{hatt2023}, and RGB versus helium-burning discrimination \citep{sreenivas2026}. Here
$\nu_{\max}$ is the frequency of maximum oscillation power. Detection tasks are scored by the area
under the precision--recall curve (PR-AUC), whose floor is the positive-class prevalence rather than
$0.5$; prevalence is therefore quoted with every detection score. The two regressions are scored by
$R^{2}$.

\paragraph{Uncertainties.} All error bars are $2\times$ the standard error of the mean over seeds.
For the $\mu$ arms the seeds are independent pre-training runs (six); for C1 and C2 they are
initialisation and shuffling seeds (six, matching the pre-training count). Because a pre-training seed
and an initialisation seed are different objects, differences between a $\mu$ arm and a supervised arm
are treated as unpaired and their bands added in quadrature even at equal counts. The engineered arm is
deterministic and carries no seed spread.
A difference counts as resolved only when it exceeds the combined $2\,\mathrm{SE}$ band. These bands
measure re-training spread only; a paired bootstrap over the test stars (1{,}000 resamples, every arm
rescored on the same stars, seeds averaged; Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}) excludes zero on 5 of the 7
fusion gains, 5 of the 6 leads over C1 and 7 of the 9 over C2, the rest stay ahead in at least 65\%
of resamples, none changes sign, and the rows that loosen are the two 150--161-star tasks.

\paragraph{The learned features add to the engineered features.} Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} and
Figure~\ref{fig:deltas}a give the comparison. Concatenating $\mu$ with the 25 features improves the
frozen readout beyond $2\,\mathrm{SE}$ on 7 of 10 tasks, led by solar-like oscillators $+0.071$,
pulsating $+0.057$ and flare activity $+0.051$, and reaching $+0.013$ ($R^{2}$) on rotation period.
Rotation is positive but just short of the
bar ($+0.016$ against $0.016$). The two negative tasks are the ones where the engineered basis is
already saturated or the probe is small: oscillating giants ($-0.003$, on a baseline of $0.920$) and
RGB versus helium-burning ($-0.051$, 161 test stars). On both, an untrained-encoder control loses by an amount we cannot resolve from the trained
fusion's -- the signature of extra collinear columns diluting a linear fit, not of $\mu$ carrying
misleading content; Appendix~\ref{app:negatives} gives both rows in full. Across all ten tasks the untrained control is negative on seven
and never exceeds $+0.016$, so the gains are specific to what pre-training learned. $\mu$ on its own
does not replace the features: it is ahead on only 4 of 10 tasks alone, which is the same conclusion
\citet{li2025staremb} reach for foundation-model embeddings, and is why we report fusion rather than
substitution \citep{makinen2024}.

\paragraph{The frozen pipeline matches or exceeds the two supervised networks.} Against C1 -- the same
convolutional trunk, the same labels, the same input windows, trained end-to-end -- the frozen fusion
probe is ahead on 6 of 10 tasks and indistinguishable from it on the other 4
(Figure~\ref{fig:deltas}b). Against
C2, an MLP on raw flux, it is ahead on 9 of 10 and behind on one (RGB versus helium-burning, 161
stars). C1 is not weak: it clears the engineered arm on 5 of 10 and the $\mu$-only arm on 4, and on
seven tasks the frozen probe recovers $0.98$--$1.09$ of its margin above the metric floor.
This is a statement about this architecture on 669 to 16{,}002 labelled stars per task, not about
supervision in general.

\paragraph{The latent-dynamics term is what buys the complementarity.} Replacing the
forward--backward dynamics objective with a dynamics-free variant, all else fixed and the same six
seeds, reduces the fusion gain on all 10 tasks, and by more than $2\,\mathrm{SE}$ on 6 when the two
arms are differenced seed by seed (Figure~\ref{fig:deltas}a, open triangles; largest losses solar-like
oscillators $-0.047$, rotation $-0.026$, $\nu_{\max}$ $-0.025$). The prediction term, not the
autoencoder alone, supplies what the engineered features lack.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Model selection must be gated on a held-out probe.} Model selection, not optimisation, is
where this pipeline is fragile. The reconstruction objective develops a localised artifact at the
window boundary, and none of eleven interventions satisfied both pre-registered acceptance criteria,
so the tapered variant shipped on the stated rule, not because it won. Underlying this is one measurement (Appendix~\ref{app:valloss}). Across ten pre-training
recipes of one sweep, validation reconstruction \emph{anti}-predicts downstream probe score: the
Spearman $\rho$ between negated validation reconstruction and PR-AUC runs from $-0.58$ to $-0.84$ over
four tasks, the loss-optimal recipe is the dynamics-free one on all four, and trusting the loss costs
$0.021$--$0.049$ PR-AUC. In the recipe family we ship the sign is mixed ($\rho$ from $-0.51$ to
$+0.37$): there the loss does not predict the probe, and in neither family does it pick the
probe-optimal recipe. Across seeds of one recipe no label-free metric we tried ranks anything.
Self-supervised representation work here therefore cannot be tuned on its own objective and must be
gated on a held-out probe.

\paragraph{\textbf{Limitations and next steps.}} The four linear-readout arms (\texttt{features},
$\mu$, \texttt{features}\,$\oplus\,\mu$, and the untrained control) share one linear model. It has few
parameters, so it cannot rescue weak features, and it is identical across the four, so the comparison
is between feature sets. Our claim is made for these 25 engineered features and a linear readout;
we make no claim about other feature sets or more powerful readouts. Our corpus is the bright ($T < 10$) 2-minute sample, and every arm, the two
supervised baselines included, sees one 5.7-day segment per star. The fainter, far more numerous
stars in the full-frame images, and light curves spanning several sectors (which would let rotation
periods longer than 5.7 days be measured), are the obvious next experiments. Our
negatives are stars absent from the label catalogues, not stars verified to lack the signal, so a
correct detection the catalogue missed counts against us; every detection score is therefore a lower
bound. The flare label marks stars that have flared somewhere in their TESS data, not a flare inside
the 5.7-day segment we encode, so the task is recognising a flaring star, not finding a flare. When
the readout is trained on one percent of the labels, the advantage narrows on 6 of 10 tasks, so how
much pre-training helps when labels are scarce remains an open question.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and disclosure.} \S2 gives the corpus cut, geometry, features, objective
and readouts; the encoder has four convolutional stages of width $32/64/128/256$ (kernel 5, each
halving length), a 128-dimensional Gaussian latent and a single-layer GRU of width 256. Splits are by
star, drawn once and shared by every arm, the supervised baselines included. Code and
score tables will be released on publication; generative AI assisted with analysis code and prose editing.

\paragraph{Oscillating giants: the engineered basis is already near-sufficient.} The labels of
\citet{hon2021} come from a convolutional classifier reading a $128 \times 128$ image of each star's
power spectrum, which fires on a visible oscillation power excess at
$6 \lesssim \nu_{\max} \lesssim 250\,\mu$Hz. Our engineered vector encodes close to that same
quantity: eight log-spaced band powers spanning 0.3--360\,c/d, plus the spectral centroid and
entropy, locate the excess and describe its concentration. The features accordingly reach $0.920$,
and the trained and untrained fusion arms then lose the same $0.003$ -- 128 collinear columns
diluting a linear fit, with no trained-versus-untrained difference left to attribute to $\mu$.

\paragraph{RGB versus helium-burning: no arm resolves the discriminant.} \citet{sreenivas2026} assign
the evolutionary state with a convolutional classifier reading the background-corrected power spectrum
\emph{folded at} $\Delta\nu$; the physics underneath is the dipole mixed-mode period spacing
$\Delta\Pi_{1}$ \citep{bedding2011}. Neither is accessible at our baseline. The encoder sees one
256-cadence window at a time, so the finest frequency structure available to $\mu$ is
$\Delta f = 32.6\,\mu$Hz, while $\Delta\nu$ for these stars is $\approx 1.5$--$18\,\mu$Hz -- the comb
itself is unresolved, let alone a fold of it. The engineered features, computed on the whole 5.69\,d
segment, reach $2.0\,\mu$Hz, still coarser than the mixed-mode spacing
$\Delta\Pi_{1}\nu^{2} \approx 0.2$--$0.6\,\mu$Hz near $\nu_{\max} = 50\,\mu$Hz. No arm on this row
measures the quantity the label encodes; the $0.758$ the features reach rides on correlates of
evolutionary state rather than on its discriminant. The row behaves accordingly: it is the only one
where C2 ($0.762$) is ahead of the frozen probe while C1 reaches only $0.530$ on 755 training stars,
and it carries the smallest test set in the study.

Figure~\ref{fig:valloss} is the measurement behind the model-selection paragraph of \S4. In row (a)
the loss-optimal recipe is the dynamics-free one (red cross) on all four tasks, and it is the worst or
next-to-worst probe on each, so selecting on the loss costs $0.021$--$0.049$ PR-AUC against the
probe-optimal recipe. The trend is not carried by that one point: with it removed, $\rho$ still runs
from $-0.43$ to $-0.87$. Those recipes are an earlier sweep than the shipped encoder (they carry an
earlier spectral auxiliary term), so row (b) repeats the estimator on the family we ship: 19 recipes
built on the final encoder that vary the form, weight and floor of the auxiliary term, six seeds each
(two more recipes in which one seed collapsed are excluded; we never drop a seed). There the
anti-correlation does not persist as such: $\rho$ is $-0.51$ on pulsating and $-0.50$ on transit,
$+0.37$ on eclipsing binaries and $+0.11$ on rotation. What persists is that the loss is not a
selector: its optimum is again the recipe with the weakest competing term (the diamond, no auxiliary
term at all), which is never the probe optimum, and trusting it costs $0.011$--$0.034$ PR-AUC. So
row (a) shows an anti-correlation on every task; in row (b) it survives on two tasks and reverses on
two; in neither does the loss choose. Both are statements across recipes only: within one recipe, across its seeds, no label-free
curve metric we tried (reconstruction, reconstruction plus auxiliary, active latent units) ranks the
seeds, every such correlation sitting within $\pm 0.3$ of zero.

\section{Paired bootstrap over test stars}
\label{app:bootstrap}

The $2\,\mathrm{SE}$ bands in the body come from re-training and say nothing about whether the
particular test stars favoured one arm. For every printed contrast we therefore resample each task's
test set with replacement 1{,}000 times, rescore every arm on the identical resampled list from its
saved per-star predictions (no model is retrained), average each arm over its seeds, and take the
difference. Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap} gives the 95\% percentile interval of that difference and the
share of resamples in which the fusion arm is ahead; intervals that exclude zero are bold. The check
tightens no verdict and reverses none: every row the body calls ahead has the fusion arm ahead in at
least 65\% of resamples. It does loosen the smallest tasks -- rotation period (150 test stars) and
RGB versus helium-burning (161) account for four of the ten rows where a resolved $2\,\mathrm{SE}$
verdict becomes an interval that touches zero.
